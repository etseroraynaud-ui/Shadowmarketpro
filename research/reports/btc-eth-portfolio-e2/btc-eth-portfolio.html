1Scope and method

Two sleeves run the same Shock Engine: the adaptive-volatility 15-minute preset (two parameter sets, calm and agitated, selected by a causal daily volatility regime), with full entry signals, except that a short entry after a shock is only allowed when the daily trend regime, computed causally on closed days, is bearish. The preset was selected on Bitcoin; Ethereum runs it unchanged (identical parameter hash c6ac30464bce14dd… for both sleeves).

Common period

Rule: first UTC day on which both strategies are simulated after warm-up (ETH simulation start) → last complete UTC day covered by both. Result: 2018-09-01 → 2026-09-30, 2,952 days (8.08 years). BTC, ETH and the portfolio are all measured on exactly these days.

Capital

Initial capital 100. At t0 the BTC sleeve receives 50 and the ETH sleeve 50. Each sleeve compounds only its own capital: it opens every position with 100 % of its own equity, one position at a time, without leverage. The portfolio value is the sum of the two sleeves:

VP(t) = 50 · EBTC(t) / EBTC(t0) + 50 · EETH(t) / EETH(t0)

where E is each strategy's equity as computed by the validated backtest. The engine sizes positions as a fraction of equity without lot rounding, so a sleeve of 50 behaves exactly like the strategy's own account rescaled. Neither sleeve ever uses the other's capital: when both are invested, gross notional exposure is about 100 % of the portfolio, not 200 %. Measured exposure: average gross 17.0%, maximum 103.5% (above 100 % only through price moves against open shorts and the entry commission).

Aggregation and conventions

  • Daily returns: UTC close to close, using the equity of the last 15-minute bar of each day (bars are stamped at their open, so the 23:45 bar closes at midnight). No interpolation; both sleeves have a bar on every day of the common period.
  • Portfolio return: (VBTC(t) + VETH(t)) / (VBTC(t−1) + VETH(t−1)) − 1, i.e. sleeve returns weighted by the previous close's drifting weights. Returns are never added across sleeves.
  • Sharpe, Sortino and volatility: daily returns, √365.25 annualization (24/7 market), risk-free rate 0, population standard deviation. Validated reports quote a Sharpe on 15-minute returns; both conventions are reconciled in section 2.
  • Costs: exactly the validated assumptions. Commission 0.045 % per order on every entry and exit fill. Slippage 0 (as in the validated BTC and ETH runs). No funding, no leverage.
  • Official portfolio: A, 50/50 at t0, independent sleeves, no rebalancing, decided before any result. B (monthly rebalancing) is a diagnostic; C (equal risk) is exploratory.

A is the official portfolio and stays so whatever this table shows. B and C were defined before the results as mechanical diagnostics. They are not candidates to replace A after seeing them.

What rebalancing does here. Without rebalancing, the sleeve that compounds faster takes a growing share of the capital. ETH gained 210% between September and December 2018, so the BTC weight fell to 28% by the end of 2018. Over the period it ranged from 16% to 50%, with an average of 30%, and ended at 17%. Variant A was therefore on average an ETH-overweight portfolio, with less diversification than 50/50. Monthly rebalancing (B) puts the weights back to 50/50 at each month end. This has two effects. It keeps the mix closer to the diversified point (A held on average 70% in ETH, the more volatile sleeve). And it systematically sells the sleeve that has just outperformed to buy the other, which adds return when relative performance does not trend. Both effects helped in this sample: volatility 25.1% against 27.6%, CAGR 58.5% against 61.6%, Sharpe 1.96 against 1.88. That is a property of this history, not a rule. Its cost is negligible: 0.002% a year, because only the part of each transfer that sits in an open position has to be traded.

C (exploratory / secondary): the BTC weight is proportional to 1/σBTC, with σ the volatility of daily sleeve returns over the 90 days ending at the rebalancing close (causal). Weights sum to 1, there is no leverage, and the allocation is 50/50 until 90 days of history exist. The BTC weight ranged from 31% to 78% (median 57%). Its result is close to B's.

Why a benchmark. Selecting days on returns changes a correlation mechanically, even when the dependence does not change (truncation bias). Keeping only days when BTC lost, for example, shrinks BTC's range and lowers the measured correlation. The benchmark applies the same selection to 400 simulated histories that keep each sleeve's actual daily returns (each simulated series is a permutation of the real one) and join them with a Gaussian copula calibrated to the full-sample correlation (copula parameter 0.53). A value inside the band is what constant dependence would produce; above it means losses are more joint than that. Selections on dates (drawdowns, spot stress) are not return-conditioned in the same way and are compared with the full-period 0.36 and the 90-day rolling range. "Portfolio < 0" uses the average portfolio weight for the benchmark; with actual weights the observed Pearson is 0.05. Market stress is defined only from spot prices: crash days are the worst 5 % of the equal-weight BTC+ETH spot return (≤ −5.6%), and the bear-market filter is the same index more than 30 % below its trailing 365-day high.

Definition. A portfolio trade is one sleeve trade, since the sleeves trade different instruments and never net, measured as log(1 + PnL / portfolio equity at entry). Daily: log(1 + daily return). Share = sum of the top k values / sum of all values, which is the total log growth; above 100 % means the remaining trades or days lose in aggregate. "CAGR without top 5 %" removes those log contributions from the realized growth. Overlapping positions make trade contributions approximately, not exactly, additive, so the day-based figures are given as a check. In currency (not log) terms, the top 5 % of portfolio trades carry 159% of the PnL (later trades weigh more there because equity compounds).

The edge sits in the right tail. The best 5 % of trades (66 out of 1,311) account for 136% of the portfolio's log growth. Without them, the CAGR would be −17%. This is the profile of a strategy with many small losses (69% of trades lose) and a few large winners (payoff 4.37). Combining the sleeves does not change this structure: shares are similar for BTC, ETH and the portfolio. A period without large winning trades will be a losing period for the portfolio.

17Interpretation

  1. Do the two sleeves produce two distinct return streams?

    Partly. They are the same engine on two markets whose prices are correlated at 0.82, and they share a common component: 23% of ETH entries occur within 15 minutes of a same-direction BTC entry (0.3% by chance). The overlap ends there. Strategy returns correlate at 0.36, one sleeve trades alone 19% of the time against 8% for both, and the effective number of independent bets is 1.46 out of 2. This is one strategy with a meaningful second stream, not two independent alphas.

  2. How much diversification does ETH bring?

    A meaningful amount. Against BTC alone, the Sharpe ratio rises by +29% (1.46 → 1.88) and the maximum drawdown falls from −22.3% to −19.1%. Volatility is 8% below the weighted average of the two sleeves. The diversification ratio is 1.21. This is close to, but below, what two independent sleeves of the same quality at this correlation would give. The benefit was smaller in the second half of the period: portfolio Sharpe 1.78 against 1.55 for the better sleeve, when the correlation between the sleeves was higher (0.45).

  3. Does correlation rise sharply in crises?

    No, on the measures available. Correlation is 0.34 on non-stress days and 0.53–0.38 on spot crash and bear-market days, close to the full-period 0.36. Joint losses are not more frequent than constant dependence implies. Two qualifications apply. When both sleeves lose on the same day, the sizes of their losses are more aligned than the benchmark (0.50 against 0.21). And the deepest portfolio drawdown (2026) was a joint one.

  4. Does the portfolio improve the return/risk ratio?

    Yes, historically. The portfolio is better on Sharpe (1.88), Sortino (4.25), Calmar (3.22) and maximum drawdown. Its CAGR (62%) lies between the sleeves' (41% and 72%). In the joint monthly bootstrap, the portfolio Sharpe exceeds BTC's in 99% of replications and ETH's in 91%. The 90 % interval of the portfolio Sharpe is 1.38–2.37.

  5. Does the result depend on a single period?

    Not on a single year. The portfolio return is positive in 9 of 9 calendar years and its Sharpe is above 1 in 8 of 9. The weakest year is 2023 (+22.5%). Performance is not uniform, though. The second half of the period is clearly weaker than the first: Sharpe 1.78 (2022-09-16 → 2026-09-30) against 2.03 (2018-09-01 → 2022-09-15), with a higher correlation between the sleeves (0.45 against 0.31). The most recent stretch includes the deepest, unrecovered drawdown. Starting in 2019, which leaves out ETH's +210 % in late 2018, gives 1.73 with a CAGR of 47% (diagnostic only; the official period is unchanged). The result does depend on a small number of trades: the best 5 % carry the whole net gain. The rolling 12-month Sharpe was positive 100% of the time.

  6. What are the main risks not modeled?

    See section 20: slippage and market impact (zero in the simulation, with 115× annual turnover), perpetual funding, the difference between the data venues (Bitstamp BTC/USD, Binance ETH/USDT) and the execution venue, exchange and stablecoin risk, latency and stop execution in live markets, and the fact that the BTC preset was selected on 2017–2026 data that contains the whole common period. ETH is a transfer across assets, not across time.

Assessment. Good, not excellent, diversification. On this history the 50/50 combination clearly improves risk-adjusted returns and drawdowns, and correlation did not spike in stress. But the two sleeves share a common driver, carry unequal risk (40% / 60%), and lost together in the deepest portfolio drawdown.

18Future hypotheses

Observations made while measuring the frozen portfolio. None of them changes the official portfolio. Each would need to be specified in advance and tested on data not used here (forward period).

  • Rebalancing. Monthly rebalancing (B, Sharpe 1.96) and equal risk (C, 1.89) did better than A (1.88) in this sample, mainly because A drifted to an average BTC weight of 30%. Hypothesis: a calendar rebalancing rule improves the frozen portfolio out of sample. If tested, the rule should be fixed now and judged on forward data only.
  • Shared shocks. About a quarter of ETH entries coincide with a same-direction BTC entry, and losses on days when both lose are more aligned than the benchmark. Hypothesis: simultaneous same-direction entries carry most of the common risk. Studying this would mean changing the strategy, which is out of scope here.
  • Risk balance. At 50/50 in capital, ETH carries 60% of the variance. Whether risk balance helps out of sample is the same open question as the rebalancing one; it is not answered by this history.

20Limitations and unmodeled risks

  • In-sample trend condition. The daily-trend condition on short entries was specified in October 2026, after BTC and ETH over this period had been studied. Both sleeves are therefore in-sample for that condition; it is being followed forward separately and is not an independent out-of-sample result.
  • In-sample selection for BTC. The preset was chosen on BTC 2017–2026, which contains the whole common period. The walk-forward study (section 16) supports the BTC edge out of sample from 2020, but the BTC sleeve figures here are in-sample. ETH is zero-shot across assets, not across time.
  • Execution costs. Slippage is zero and fills happen at the bar close or at the stop level. At about 230× two-way turnover a year, each 0.01 % of average slippage per fill costs about 2.3 % of equity a year. Cost ×2 lowers the portfolio Sharpe to 1.61.
  • Market impact and capacity are not modeled (section 15).
  • Funding. The simulation trades spot-like instruments without funding. Live execution on perpetual futures pays or receives funding while a position is open (about 26% of the time). Stress tests (positions charged or credited at each funding time, correct sign for shorts): historical Binance perpetual funding (2020 onwards; constant +0.01 % per 8 h before 2020): Sharpe 1.81, CAGR 58.4%, max drawdown −19.2%; same, with Hyperliquid funding from May 2023: Sharpe 1.80, CAGR 57.6%, max drawdown −19.2%; stress: shorts pay 0.01 % per 8 h at all times: Sharpe 1.86, CAGR 60.7%, max drawdown −19.2%. Slippage per order: 0.00 % → Sharpe 1.88, 0.01 % → Sharpe 1.82, 0.02 % → Sharpe 1.76, 0.05 % → Sharpe 1.58.
  • Data and venues. BTC/USD from Bitstamp and ETH/USDT from Binance. The execution venue (Hyperliquid perpetuals for the bot) has different prices, liquidity, fees and stop mechanics. ETH/USDT also carries USDT risk.
  • Live mechanics. Candle availability, latency and stop-trigger behavior observed on the live exchange can differ from the simulator's intrabar path (see the bot documentation).
  • Right-tail dependence. The best 5 % of trades carry the entire net gain. A market in which large trending moves after shocks disappear would remove the edge, and the bootstrap cannot represent such a regime.
  • Counterparty, custody and operational risks are not part of a backtest.
